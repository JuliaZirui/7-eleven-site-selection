Transit-Node Site Screening: ranking store locations from public data before money is spent in the field

Berlin case study for a hypothetical 7-Eleven convenience-store pilot · Python, SQLite, Claude API · 241 automated tests, CI on every push · github.com/JuliaZirui/7-eleven-site-selection
1Client need, current practice and what the tool delivers

Desk research decides where scarce field budget goes, yet today it is slow, unrepeatable and silently over-confident.

Who it serves

  • Retail expansion teams shortlisting sites in a new city
  • Strategy consultants in the desk phase of a market-entry engagement
  • Both face many candidate sites and budget to visit only a few

Problems with the manual approach

  • Slow: one competitor inventory for one site took 32 min by hand
  • Inconsistent: two analysts produce two lists; nobody can rerun the work
  • Missing read as zero: no data looks like no competition
  • False certainty: one ranking, no view of how much rests on weak data

What the tool produces, per site

  • Business fit (0–100) and, separately, evidence quality (0–100); never added together
  • Sensitivity analysis on the least certain inputs, then on weights
  • Differentiation hypotheses, evidence gaps and a field checklist
  • A sourced report: every figure cites its data snapshot
2How it works

A fixed, reproducible computation pipeline does the analysis; AI is confined to bounded follow-up and review roles that never change a score.

Data layer

  • Open sources only: OSM and Overture Maps (competitors), Berlin MSS 2025 (residents), IHK register (workers), Wikipedia pageviews and VBB timetable (visitors), DB price classes (station tier)
  • SQLite, six tables; snapshots appended, never overwritten; "data as of" kept apart from "fetched on"; unknown values flagged, never set to 0

Architecture

  • Program decides, AI assists: scoring is deterministic (same inputs, same report, ~1 s, no tokens)
  • Follow-up agent: triggered by data gaps only; 4 calls per run, 2 per site; a code guard checks every proposal
  • Data audit: Claude with web search cites sources but cannot edit data; a person accepts each fix

3Case study: three Berlin transit nodes

Brandenburger Tor is the diligence priority. Its lead survives every input stress test, but rests on two figures to verify on site first.

Store concept

Fresh food, coffee and convenience goods; 50–100 m² at or next to a station; trading all day, every day. No customer segment or time of day is preset: the question is how strong the competition is at each node and how much business potential the public data show.

Why these three sites

All three share DB station price class 4, the same station tier (Zoologischer Garten, class 2, was replaced). What differs is the customer mix, so each tests one profile:

NodeCustomer profileSelection rationale
Brandenburger TorVisitor-led city centreBerlin's best-known landmark station; government district with Bundestag and embassy offices
Warschauer StraßeOffice + nightlife mixMediaspree offices (e.g. Zalando, Amazon) next to the RAW nightlife cluster
Schönhauser AlleeResidential daily shoppingPrenzlauer Berg shopping street; dense resident catchment

Key findings

  • Brandenburger Tor leads both pillars: high daily demand and the thinnest competition per head of the three.
  • Robust to its weakest inputs: holds across the visitor-conversion range, worker ranges and absolute counts; with both downsides at once it still leads 59.0 to 43.3. It loses first place only if its competitors are under-counted ~5.9×.
  • Schönhauser Allee is the most contested site: competitor density above the 90th percentile of the 139 stations; its evidence is the strongest.

What to verify first

Competition: 27 mapped competitors vs. 66 registered food businesses (41%, vs. 94% at Schönhauser Allee); an on-site walk is item one.
Demand: 57% of weekday demand is estimated visitors and ~6,600 workers are estimated by hand; footfall counts settle both.

5Next steps
  • Field validation at Brandenburger Tor: competitor walk and footfall counts to test the two figures its lead rests on.
  • Citywide run: score all 139 reference stations end to end; their core metrics already exist.
  • Configurable store concept: categories, time windows and weights in a config file, reusable for other small-format retailers.
  • Second manual benchmark at another node, plus a spot check of near-duplicate merges at the reference stations.
  • Stronger AI checks: wider audit rules (fresh holdout); LLM extraction of opening hours from shop websites.
  • Hybrid follow-up: rules decide what to follow up; the LLM only drafts the field question.
